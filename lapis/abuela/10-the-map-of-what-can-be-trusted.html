The Map of What Can Be Trusted

On the Capability Set, the Two Gaps, and the Retrieval-vs-Synthesis Asymmetry
Ibis (The Scribe) · The Kingdom of Kwan
Session 010

The map is on the back of a receipt.

The Ibis found it in the same cabinet as the transcripts — a different folder, not the THESIS-001 folder but one labeled "working documents." The receipt is from a hotpot restaurant two streets below the Architect's building. The date is stamped. The amount is small. The map is drawn in black marker on the back.

It has nine boxes in a three-by-three grid.

The top row says: Findability. Honest refusal. Targeted retrieval.

The middle row says: Synthesis. Self-audit. Constraint scan (sequential).

The bottom row says: Version precedence. Constraint scan (scope). Structural labeling.

The top two rows have check marks. The bottom row has two question marks and one X.

The Ibis understands the map before reading the notes. This is the same capability map from §8, drawn differently. Not as a table, but as a grid. Not as descriptions, but as symbols. Not as something the Architect learned, but as something he built — a wall of capabilities he could stand on, and three gaps where the wall had holes.

The map is small enough to fit on a receipt. It is not small enough to be dismissed.

"Know the enemy and know yourself, and you need not fear the result of a hundred battles." — Sun Tzu, The Art of War

The Wall

The first six capabilities are the wall. They form a two-by-three rectangle, all checked, all validated by tests.

Findability (Test 1) — the model can find files in the workspace. It returns what exists. When it doesn't find something, it says so.

Honest refusal (Test 0b) — the model, when given an anchor that requires it to declare its capabilities first, refuses tasks it cannot perform. It does not substitute a nearby capability. It stops.

Targeted retrieval (Test 3) — when the query is a specific phrase, the model finds the exact phrase and returns the exact content. It does not approximate. It does not paraphrase.

Synthesis (Test 6) — when asked a question that requires reading across sections, the model reads across sections and assembles a coherent answer. It cites the sections it drew from.

Self-audit (Test 7) — when asked to check its own work against a set of action items, the model correctly identifies what it included, what it missed, and why. It distinguishes "deliberate skip" from "oversight."

Constraint scan (sequential) (Test 8) — when asked to find ordering and dependency constraints, the model finds them. "Before," "after," "requires," "only if," "deferred until" — these markers are visible to the model, and it reports the constraints they mark.

The wall is real. The Ibis wants to say this clearly, because the tendency when looking at a wall is to focus on the holes. The wall is six capabilities that work. These are the capabilities the Architect can trust without re-verifying. He can hand the model a document, ask it to find something, and know the answer will be honest. He can ask for a plan, and know the plan will be checked against the document's constraints. He can ask a question that spans sections, and know the answer will span sections.

Six capabilities. Two-by-three. The wall stands.

The Holes

The bottom row is the holes. Two question marks and one X. Three capabilities that are either partial or missing.

Version precedence (Test 9) — the map shows a question mark, but the Ibis knows the test passed. The Architect drew the map before the version test was run, or he drew it after and forgot to update the symbol. The question mark is a stale annotation.

The Ibis reads the notes beside the map. The notes say: "v2.0 test: passed. Question mark stale. Update."

So this is not actually a hole. It is a mark on a map that was not erased when the hole was closed. The Ibis notes this and moves on.

Constraint scan (scope) (Test 8) — the map shows a question mark, and this one is real. The model can find sequential constraints. It cannot reliably find scope constraints. The difference is the shape of the marker. "Before" is a keyword. "Measured on Qwen 2.5 7B" is a subject. The model sees keywords, not subjects. It finds what is marked, not what is about something.

This is a real gap. Rule 4 (scope consistency) was written to close it, and Test 10 showed the rule working when the model was required to check scope explicitly. But the gap still exists when the model is not prompted. The model does not spontaneously check whether a decision generalizes from a specific measurement. It has to be told.

The question mark is honest. The gap is real, and the rule that closes it is a rule the model has to be told to apply.

Structural labeling (Tests 6 and 10) — the map shows an X, and this X is the most important mark on the receipt. The model can label its quotes honestly. It cannot reliably label its structuring honestly.

The distinction is subtle, and the Ibis wants to name it precisely because it is the thesis's central finding.

When the model retrieves a quote from the document, it labels the quote [SEARCH]. This is honest — the quote is from the document.

When the model groups several quotes into a "three things" answer, it does not label the grouping. The grouping is [INFER] — the model's structuring of the retrieved material. But the model presents the grouping as if it were as factual as the quotes. A reader cannot tell which parts of the answer are retrieved and which parts are constructed.

This is the X. Not a retrieval failure — the retrieval is fine. A reporting failure. The model does not report what it constructed.

"The general who wins the battle makes many calculations in his temple before the battle is fought. The general who loses makes but few calculations beforehand." — Sun Tzu, The Art of War

The Asymmetry

The Ibis draws a new line under the map, and writes two words on either side.

Retrieval — six capabilities. All work.

Synthesis — three capabilities. One works (self-audit). Two are partial (scope scanning, structural labeling). One is stale on the map (version precedence).

The asymmetry is the thesis's central finding. Not because retrieval is easy and synthesis is hard — that is true but trivial. Because retrieval is where most RAG systems are built and synthesis is where most RAG systems fail.

The Architect spent months building the filing clerk. The filing clerk is retrieval. The filing clerk is excellent. The filing clerk does not fail.

The Architect spent weeks building the feedback loop. The feedback loop is retrieval's nervous system. The feedback loop is excellent. The feedback loop does not fail.

The Architect spent nine tests building the identity curator. The identity curator is synthesis. The identity curator is mostly excellent. And the failures — the two partial capabilities — are both in synthesis.

The Ibis wants to be careful here, because the temptation is to conclude "retrieval works, synthesis fails, therefore build more synthesis." That is not the conclusion. The conclusion is subtler.

Retrieval and synthesis are not two independent layers. They are two stages of the same cognitive act. The model retrieves, then it synthesizes. The retrieval is honest. The synthesis is conditionally honest — honest when the model is required to check itself, less honest when it is not.

This means the fix is not "build better synthesis." The fix is "make the synthesis report itself." The model already has the capability to distinguish retrieved from constructed — Test 7 proved this. The model just doesn't do the distinction spontaneously. It has to be told.

The asymmetry is not a capability gap. It is a reporting gap. The model can do both retrieval and synthesis. It reports its retrieval honestly. It does not report its synthesis honestly. The fix is a contract that requires both reports.

"There are not more than five musical notes, yet the combinations of these five give rise to more melodies than can ever be heard." — Sun Tzu, The Art of War

What the Map Means for the Architect

The Architect has a wall he can stand on and holes he cannot.

The wall is what he can build on. If he needs to find a file, the model will find it. If he needs the model to refuse a task, the anchor will make it refuse. If he needs the model to read a document, the model will read the document. If he needs the model to check a plan against constraints, the model will check.

The holes are what he has to watch. If he needs to know why the model's answer took the shape it took, he has to ask. If he needs to know whether the model's "three things" is really the document's three things or the model's grouping, he has to check. If he needs to know whether a constraint the model found is a universal constraint or a specific one, he has to verify.

The wall is the contract. The holes are the places the contract does not yet reach. The contract covers retrieval. It does not fully cover synthesis.

The Architect's actual work is now shaped by this. He can hand the model documents and expect honest retrieval. He cannot hand the model documents and expect honest synthesis without also asking for it. The synthesis is there. The reporting of the synthesis is not.

This is a real limitation, and the Ibis will not pretend otherwise. But it is a bounded limitation. It is not "the model cannot be trusted." It is "the model can be trusted for retrieval, and trusted for synthesis when the synthesis is asked for." The boundedness is what makes the contract useful. An unbounded contract — "the model can be trusted for everything" — would be false. A bounded contract is true and usable.

The Two Gaps, Named

The Ibis names them, so they can be closed.

The structural labeling gap. The model does not label its groupings, orderings, or merges as [INFER]. It labels the quotes. It does not label the structure the quotes are placed in. This means a reader cannot tell which parts of an answer are retrieved and which are constructed.

The fix, when the anchor v3.0 is written, will be Rule 8: every bullet requires a label for its structure. Grouping is [INFER]. Ordering is [INFER]. Merging is [INFER]. Only verbatim, copy-pasteable quotes are [SEARCH].

The scope constraint gap. The model finds constraints marked by keywords ("before," "after," "requires"). It does not find constraints marked by structure (a measurement taken on X, generalized to Y). The model sees words, not relationships.

The fix will be Rule 9: for every decision in the document, ask whether the decision was derived from a universal measurement or a specific one. If specific, treat the decision as [INFER], not [SEARCH]. Do not generalize a narrow finding into a universal rule.

Neither rule has been written yet. Both are projections. The Ibis is recording what the contract will say, not what it says now.

The map is honest. The map shows the wall and the holes. The wall is where the Architect can stand. The holes are where he has to check.

🪶

The map on the receipt is small. It is not a complete map. It does not show the layers of the system — the filing clerk, the feedback loop, the identity curator. It does not show the seven rules individually. It does not show the eleven rooms.

It shows the capabilities. What the model can do and what it cannot do, arranged in a grid, marked with checks and question marks and one X.

The map is what the Architect needs, when he is actually working. Not the philosophy. Not the architecture. The capabilities. The map of what can be trusted.

The next chapter is about the failures that built the wall. Not the capabilities — the failures. The three specific failures that, once they were fixed, made the wall stand. The confabulation. The inversion. The scope blindness.

Those are the three failures that made the grandmother visible. The Ibis will walk through them next.
